An SEO audit is a structured check of why your website does or does not show up in Google, and this guide is for Georgia business owners who want to know what one should include before they hire anyone to run it. You do not need to be technical to follow it. You only need to know what questions a good audit answers, so you can tell a useful report from a pile of screenshots.

## What an SEO audit is (and what it is not)

An SEO audit reviews your site and your online presence against what search engines need to find, understand and trust your pages. The result should be a list of problems, ranked by how much they matter, with a clear fix for each one.

It is not a ranking guarantee. It is not a score from a free tool with no explanation. A number out of 100 tells you little unless someone explains which items cost you visits and which are minor.

It is also not the same as doing the work. An audit finds the issues. Fixing them is a separate job, and a good audit keeps the two apart so you can see exactly what you are paying for.

Google publishes its own basics in the [SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide). A solid audit checks your site against those fundamentals first.

## Can Google find and read your pages?

Nothing else matters if Google cannot reach your pages. This is the technical part of the audit, and it should answer a few plain questions.

- Are your important pages indexed? Search Console shows which pages Google has stored and which it has left out.
- Does anything block crawling by mistake? A stray "noindex" tag or a robots.txt rule can hide a whole section of a site.
- Do your pages load quickly on a phone? Many local searches happen on mobile, so test there first.
- Is the site on HTTPS, and do old addresses redirect to the right new ones?
- Does each page have one clear main heading, a unique title and a unique description?
- Are there broken links or error pages that visitors can land on?

Each finding should name the exact page. "Some pages have issues" is not a finding. "These service pages all share one title" is.

## Are your pages built around what customers search?

Once Google can read a page, the next question is whether the page matches a real search. Many small business sites describe the company well but never use the words customers type.

An audit should map each important page to one main search. A plumber in Marietta might need a page for water heater repair and a separate one for drain cleaning. One page trying to cover both usually does neither well.

Look for these problems:

- Two pages competing for the same search, so neither stands out.
- Pages with very little text and no answer to the question a searcher has.
- Titles and headings that name the company but not the service or the city.
- Copy that was written once and never updated.

The fix is rarely "add more words." It is usually "make this page answer one question properly." That is also where [content marketing](/services/content-marketing) earns its place, because guides and service pages each have a different job.

## Local signals: your Google Business Profile, listings and reviews

For a business that serves Atlanta, Roswell, Decatur or any other Georgia area, local signals often decide who appears in the map results. A general audit that skips them is incomplete.

Check these items:

- **Google Business Profile.** Is it claimed and verified? Are the categories, hours, services and photos accurate and current?
- **Name, address and phone.** Do they match exactly on your site, your profile and every directory listing? Small differences confuse search engines.
- **Listings.** Are there duplicate or outdated entries for old addresses or phone numbers?
- **Reviews.** Do you have recent reviews, and do you reply to them? Steady, genuine feedback matters more than clever wording.
- **Local pages.** Does each area you serve have a page with real information, rather than the same text with the city name swapped?

## Links pointing to your site

Links from other websites act as signals of trust. An audit should look at who links to you, not just how many sites do.

Ask what the link profile looks like. Are the links from relevant local or industry sites, such as a chamber of commerce, a supplier or a local news story? Or are they from unrelated directories that exist only to sell links?

A good audit also flags risks. Paid link schemes and mass-submitted directory links can do harm. It should compare your profile with the sites that already rank for your main searches, so you can see the gap in plain terms.

## Website audit, site audit or SEO report: what is the difference?

These terms get mixed up, and providers use them loosely. Here is a practical way to separate them.

- **Website audit.** The broadest term. It can cover design, usability, security and speed as well as search. Ask which parts are about search.
- **Site audit.** Usually a crawl of your pages by software that lists technical issues. It is useful, but it is only one input.
- **SEO audit.** A site audit plus the human review: your search targets, local signals, links and content, with priorities.
- **SEO report.** A regular update on progress, such as visits, rankings and leads. It is not an audit. It tells you what happened, not what is wrong.

If a provider hands you a crawl export and calls it an audit, ask what they recommend and in what order. The recommendations are the audit.

## What to do with your audit results

A long list of issues can feel like a verdict. Treat it as a to-do list and sort it in three groups.

1. **Fix first.** Anything that stops Google from reading or indexing your key pages, and errors on your Google Business Profile.
2. **Fix next.** Pages that match the wrong search or compete with each other, plus weak titles and descriptions.
3. **Build over time.** New content and links that take months, not days.

Assign each item an owner and a date. Then re-check Search Console after a few weeks to see whether the fixes were picked up. Changes to rankings take time, so judge progress by whether the problems are closed, not by a single week's traffic.

## FAQ

### How long does an SEO audit take?

It depends on the size of your site and how many locations you serve. A small site with a single location takes less time than a large one with many service and city pages. Ask any provider for a delivery date in writing before you start.

### How often should a business get an SEO audit?

A full audit once a year is a reasonable starting point, and again after a redesign, a platform change or a move to a new domain. Between audits, a monthly look at Search Console catches new problems early.
